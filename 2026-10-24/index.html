Foster + Partners draws a garden-bridge skyline for Yerevan

Two towers joined by three enclosed bridges anchor the planned World Trade Center Yerevan.

Read the briefing +

Armenia's capital is getting a pair of skyscrapers designed around public space rather than against it.

  • What happened: Foster + Partners unveiled World Trade Center Yerevan, a development centred on two skyscrapers connected by three enclosed bridge elements.
  • Details: One tower holds offices, the other apartments and a hotel. The glazed pair is angled toward Mount Ararat, the Opera and the Genocide Memorial, and sits on low-rise buildings arranged around a public square, with half the site kept free at ground level.
  • Why it matters: The scheme works inside the garden-city district masterplanned by Alexander Tamanian in 1924, extending a park axis to the podium and tying an events plaza to the forecourt of the St John the Baptist Church.
  • Caveat: This is a revealed design with renders. Foster + Partners has not published a construction timeline.

AS+GG's Lilli brings a softened silhouette to Miami's waterfront

The studio behind Jeddah Tower designs a 636-foot residential tower for Edgewater.

Read the briefing +

Miami's next luxury tower trades glass-box sharpness for rounded edges and bay-facing terraces.

  • What happened: Adrian Smith + Gordon Gill Architecture unveiled Lilli, a 636-foot (193-metre) residential skyscraper planned for a narrow site in Miami's Edgewater neighbourhood.

  • Caveat: Lilli is an unveiled design, not a finished building, and it enters a crowded Miami luxury residential pipeline.

Loader Monteith squeezes a quart into a pint pot near Manchester

A 69-square-metre bungalow becomes a four-bedroom family home without losing its brick character.

Read the briefing +

Small-house architecture is where British studios do some of their most inventive work.

  • What happened: Scottish studio Loader Monteith transformed a two-bedroom red-brick bungalow in Prestwich, near Manchester, into a four-bedroom family home called Deyne Avenue.
  • Details: The 69-square-metre house gained a rear brick extension that opens the living spaces to the garden, plus a new upper floor tucked beneath a dormer roof. The studio describes the brief as squeezing a quart into a pint pot. Jake Balston photographed it.
  • Why it matters: The project shows how much life a compact post-war bungalow can hold when the extension works in the same material as the original house.
  • Caveat: A single-house project, so its lessons are about craft rather than scale.

A three-month theatre becomes a permanent home in Hackney Wick

Takero Shimazaki Architects rebuilds The Yard Theatre with reclaimed materials and a beacon tower.

Read the briefing +

London's scrappiest theatre just got its forever building, and it kept the scrapyard soul.

  • What happened: Takero Shimazaki Architects revamped and extended The Yard Theatre in London's Hackney Wick, turning a venue built in 2011 as a three-month project into a permanent theatre.
  • Details: The redesign adds a bar, backstage facilities and community space, built largely with reclaimed materials. A crane tower on site becomes a soaring marker for the venue, and the new hall has been described as church-like. Philip Vile photographed the project.
  • Why it matters: The Yard's original warehouse conversion by Practice Architecture was never meant to last. Making it permanent without erasing its makeshift character is a neat case study in upgrading London's grassroots culture infrastructure.
  • Caveat: Coverage so far is the architect's presentation of the finished project; programming continuity will be the real test.

Design Miami Paris fills a Lagerfeld mansion with 140 years of design

The fair's fourth edition runs at L'Hotel de Maisons until Sunday.

Read the briefing +

Paris's collectible-design fair opened days after unrest in the city, and the courtyards are the reason to go.

  • What happened: Design Miami Paris 2026 is on view at L'Hotel de Maisons until 25 October, spanning 140 years of design and decorative arts across the 18th-century mansion where Karl Lagerfeld once lived.
  • Details: The courtyard holds a huge bronze bear by Francois-Xavier Lalanne from Galerie Mitterrand and a three-faced wooden pavilion by Michele De Lucchi for Volumnia.   • Why it matters: The fourth edition confirms Paris as the fair's most atmospheric setting, with dealers and collectors showing up despite a week of riots in the city.
  • Caveat: This is one magazine's selection of highlights, and the fair closes tomorrow, 25 October.

A 1944 Prouve house lands in Paris as a Japanese tea room

Galerie Patrick Seguin reimagines the 6x6 Demountable House as a chashitsu.

Read the briefing +

The most quietly radical object at the fair is a house designed to be built in a day.

  • What happened: Galerie Patrick Seguin brought Jean Prouve's 1944 6x6 Demountable House to Design Miami Paris, installing it in the gardens of the Hotel de Maisons with an interior reimagined as a Japanese tea ceremony pavilion.
  • Details: Prouve designed the demountable house at the end of the Second World War as emergency housing for displaced families; three people can assemble it in a single day. The tea-room reading nods to Prouve's lifelong ties to Japan and his collaborator Reiko Hayama.
  • Why it matters: The installation connects wartime emergency architecture to the quiet ritual of the chashitsu, a rare piece of design history you can walk inside.
  • Caveat: The house is for sale through the gallery, so the presentation is also a sales showcase. It is on show only until 25 October.

Verner Panton's Panthella grows two new floor lamps

Louis Poulsen revives the 1971 lamp's five original opal colours and adds a smaller size.

Read the briefing +

In the centenary year of Panton's birth, his softest light gets its fullest family yet.

  • What happened: Louis Poulsen added two floor lamps to Verner Panton's Panthella family: the Panthella 500 The Originals, in the five original opal colours, and a new, smaller Panthella 400 in opal white.
  • Details: Panton designed Panthella with Louis Poulsen in 1971 so that both stand and shade act as reflectors, giving a glare-free glow from base and shade. The 500 stands 1,290 millimetres tall in opal blue grey, red, orange, green and brown; the 400 is 1,035 millimetres in white.
  • Why it matters: The year 2026 marks a century since Panton's birth, and the revival of the original colour set restores the lamp as he first imagined it rather than as the all-white classic it became.
  • Caveat: This is a manufacturer announcement, so treat the heritage framing as marketing, though the design history checks out.

Art Basel Paris shuts its weekend over protest fears

After brisk sales, the fair closed Friday evening and stays closed Saturday and Sunday.

Read the briefing +

The biggest art story of the weekend is the fair you can no longer visit.

  • What happened: Art Basel Paris announced on Thursday afternoon that it would close at 7 pm on Friday 23 October and remain closed on Saturday and Sunday, citing planned demonstrations and the safety of visitors, staff and artworks.
  • Details: The decision followed social media calls to assemble in front of the Grand Palais on Saturday. Before the shutdown, galleries reported selling more than 300 works for about 147 million dollars across the first two preview days, according to figures compiled by the fair. White Cube sold two Julie Mehretu paintings at 7.85 million dollars each, Gagosian placed a de Kooning above 10 million, and the fair's priciest work, a 1917 Modigliani nude at Pace Di Donna Schrader, was listed above 100 million. Franceinfo confirms the Louvre, Musee d'Orsay, the Orangerie, the Picasso museum and the Grand Palais all closed on Saturday 24 October, after the Culture Ministry told institutions to protect visitors, staff and artworks; the Louvre's own site confirms the closure. AP reports 70,000 police officers deployed across France.
  • Why it matters: Weekend days are when fairs open to the public; last year Art Basel Paris drew more than 73,000 visitors across all days. Closing them turns a market success into a civic story about Paris itself.
  • Caveat: This is a developing situation and the sales figures are self-reported by galleries and the fair.

Tate Modern traces how photography fought its way into art

Light and Magic runs until February, with razor blades, photomontage and five Guardian stars.

Read the briefing +

Before photography was art, its pioneers used every trick in the darkroom to prove it could be.

  • What happened: Light and Magic: The Birth of Art Photography is running at Tate Modern until 21 February 2027, telling the story of pictorialism and the photographers who claimed the medium as fine art.
  • Details: The show spans Yevonde's society portraits, Edward Weston's 1927 Shells, William Mortensen's prints reworked with razor blades and inks, Ho Fan's misty 1959 Hong Kong streets, Olive Cotton's Teacup Ballet and Iwata Nakayama's experimental photomontages.
  • Why it matters: The Guardian gave the exhibition five stars, and its global spread, from Singapore to Sydney to London, rescues pictorialism from its footnote status between painting and modern photography.
  • Caveat: The run is long, to February 2027, so there is no urgency beyond wanting a quiet room with beautiful prints.

A rammed-earth hotel rises from the soil of Valle de Guadalupe

Mira Earth Studios builds with the valley's own granite earth, four to six days per wall.

Read the briefing +

Baja California's wine country gains a boutique stay where the landscape is the client.

  • What happened: Mira Earth Studios, a boutique hotel in Mexico's Valle de Guadalupe, is featured in Architectural Digest's Iconos del Diseno 2026 for an architecture that integrates the site's slopes and stones rather than correcting them.
  • Details: Designed by Nicaraguan architect Juniette Bermudez with a local team including engineer Felipe de Jesus Ricalde, the hotel's walls were rammed from granite earth excavated on site, layered and compacted over four to six days per wall.   • Why it matters: Bermudez's line that the land and nature are the protagonists while the architects are guests captures a serious approach to building in one of Mexico's most fragile wine landscapes. Cesar Bejar photographed it.
  • Caveat: A design-award feature rather than a stay review; comfort and service are untested in this coverage.

British Journal of Photography suspends print after 172 years

The magazine founded in 1854 moves to digital-only, calling the decision a pause.

Read the briefing +

One of the world's oldest photography magazines just stopped the presses, at least for now.

  • What happened: British Journal of Photography suspended its print edition after 172 years and moved to digital-only publishing, citing unsustainable printing and distribution costs, in a letter from CEO Mick Moore.
  • Details: Founded in 1854 as a technical weekly, the magazine became a monthly fine-art title under editor Simon Bainbridge from 2010, anchored 1854 Media's awards business, and was rescued from closure by a sale in 2022. Its latest issue, Politics, covers drone warfare in Ukraine and the French far right.
  • Why it matters: The suspension ends a print run stretching back to the Victorian era and shows the pressure on specialist arts magazines even when the editorial product is strong.
  • Caveat: The magazine frames this as a pause, not a closure, but gave no timetable for print's return.

A new book makes the case against architecture's fear of colour

Chromatic Architecture collects 34 colourful buildings to cure the profession's chromophobia.

Read the briefing +

Why did modern buildings go white, and what would it take to bring colour back?

  • What happened: Chromatic Architecture: The Craft of Colour in Design, by Adam Nathaniel Furman and Kate Mazade, is out from RIBA Publishing, 208 pages of argument and case studies.
  • Details: The book gathers 34 colourful projects from the Middle Ages to the present, from Gaudi's tiled Casa Vicens to Furman's own Croydon Colonnade, and traces how twentieth-century modernism made austere white the default despite precedents like Le Corbusier's polychrome balconies.
  • Why it matters: The authors channel Owen Jones's line that form without colour is like a body without a soul, and Jacqueline Lichtenstein's scholarship on why colour gets dismissed as superficial, making the book a manifesto as much as a survey.
  • Caveat: Coverage so far is an excerpt and review in the architecture trade press; the book costs about 60 dollars.
